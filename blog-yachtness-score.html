Yachtness Score

Published July 22, 2026 · 10:57 PM EDT · by Felipe Maia Polo

I made a free web app called Yachtness Score that listens to any song and rates it from 0 to 100 on one very specific question: how much does this sound like yacht rock?

First, what is yacht rock?

Yacht rock is the smooth, studio-polished soft rock of roughly 1976–1984 — think Steely Dan, Michael McDonald, and Christopher Cross. Warm Rhodes pianos, immaculate session-musician playing, jazzy chords, and not a rough edge in sight. The name is a joke about the kind of breezy, expensive-sounding music you might play on a boat, and it has since become a genuine fan obsession.

The definitive authority is the podcast Yacht or Nyacht?, whose hosts rate songs on a 0–100 scale and draw the line at 50: above it is Yacht Rock, below it is "Nyacht." Their ratings are lovingly argued and gloriously subjective — which makes them perfect training data.

The idea

The hosts have rated thousands of songs by ear. Could a model learn to hear what they hear? Yachtness Score is my attempt: search for any song and it returns a calibrated probability, dressed up as a 0–100 score with a verdict running from LANDLOCKED to MAXIMUM YACHT — and a hand-written one-liner for every single score. "Take On Me" scores 3: "If smoothness were water this would be a desert. Nyacht."

How it works

The pipeline is deliberately simple, and every number below comes from the app's own method page:

  • Ground truth: ~1,900 songs rated by the Yacht or Nyacht? hosts, treating each song's average host score as the label.
  • Audio: a 30-second preview from Deezer, matched only when artist and title agree.
  • Features: the clip goes through MERT-v1-95M, a music foundation model. All 13 of its transformer layers are mean-pooled over time, giving a 9,984-dimensional description of how the recording sounds.
  • Classifier: an ensemble of 5 L2-regularised logistic regressions — one per cross-validation fold — whose calibrated probabilities are averaged into the final P(yacht).

That's it: a strong audio encoder, a boring linear model on top, and honest cross-validation so a song is never scored by a model that trained on it.

Does it actually work?

Reasonably well, for such a fuzzy target. On held-out songs it gets the Yacht/Nyacht call right about 70% of the time (versus 51% for always guessing the majority class), ranks a random yacht song above a random non-yacht song 77% of the time (ROC AUC 0.766), and its probability tracks the hosts' full 0–100 scores at a Pearson correlation of 0.55 — even though it was only trained on a yes/no label. It recovers more than it was asked to.

What it gets right — and wrong

It is confident and correct at the extremes. Genuine yacht rock lands high; aggressive, lo-fi, or acoustic music lands low. The trouble is the middle of the scale, where a score near 50 is close to a coin flip — exactly the songs the hosts themselves argue about.

Its most charming failure is that it over-rates polished non-yacht music. Japanese city pop, slick modern R&B, and immaculately produced boogie can all fool it, because it hears the production — the Rhodes, the reverb, the session-grade smoothness — rather than the genre or the era. It judges sound, not category.

It's a rough detector, not a verdict. A high score means "this sounds like the stuff the hosts like," not "this is yacht rock." When in doubt, trust the podcast, not the robot.

Try it

Search a song you love and see where it lands. It's free, a little silly, and — I hope — occasionally insightful. Ratings belong to the Yacht or Nyacht? hosts; the site is an unaffiliated hobby project.
