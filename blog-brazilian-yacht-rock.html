The Brazilian Yacht Rock Leaderboard

Published July 22, 2026 · 10:58 PM EDT · by Felipe Maia Polo

Brazil spent the late '70s and '80s perfecting its own strain of smooth — boogie, MPB, and jazz-funk built on the same Rhodes pianos, session players, and immaculate production as the yacht rock canon. So I asked a question: if there were a playlist called Brazilian Yacht Rock, what would be on it — and how yachty is it, really?

The experiment

The setup was a small collaboration between two very different kinds of model. First I asked a large language model to imagine a 1,000-song "Brazilian Yacht Rock" playlist. It returned a candidate list of 1,177 songs, each tagged with its own genre label ("Core Boogie", "Smooth MPB", "Soul Suave", and so on).

Then I matched those candidates against Deezer's catalogue with strict artist-and-title verification — fuzzy matches discarded rather than risked — which left 1,028 songs (87%) with a real 30-second preview. Every one of them went through the exact same model that powers Yachtness Score. The top 50 are now live on the leaderboard.

The angle, in one line: the AI curated the candidates, but the model was the judge.

Who won

The two yachtiest songs are Carlos Bivar — "Maré" (88) and Djavan — "Miragem" (88). And then there's Djavan again. And again. He takes four of the top six spots (Miragem, Infinito, Topázio, Lilás) — which makes a decent case for Djavan as Brazil's Michael McDonald. Ed Motta and Marcos Valle feature heavily just below.

One entry is special: Marcos Valle's "Estrelar" (77) is one of only five songs on the board the Yacht or Nyacht? hosts have actually rated — and it was also the AI's number-one pick. The model, the humans, and the language model all liked it. Consensus is rare here.

Most nominees aren't yacht — and that's fine

Here's the first honest finding. The AI was asked for songs that could belong on a Brazilian yacht rock playlist, and it cast a wide net. The model, listening to the actual audio, was far stricter: the median score is just 18, about 57% of songs score 20 or below, and under 2% clear 70. This isn't a playlist of yacht rock; it's a pile of candidates with a thin, genuinely smooth top layer.

Does the AI's taxonomy match the model's ears?

Surprisingly often, yes. When you group songs by the genre label the language model assigned and look at what the audio model thought, the ordering is intuitive: "Core Boogie" sits highest (median 35), smooth and jazz-funk categories cluster in the middle, and acoustic or aggressive buckets — Rap/Funk, Tropicália, Honorary Bossa — sink to the bottom. The two models never spoke to each other, yet they broadly agree on which styles sound yachty.

The interesting part: the AI knew which songs, not which would win

The language model also gave each candidate a rank — its own 1-to-1,177 ordering of how central it was to the idea. If the AI truly had "ears," its ranking should predict the model's scores. It barely does: the correlation between the AI's rank and the audio score is essentially flat (r ≈ −0.12).

That gap is the whole story. The AI was excellent at nominating plausible songs — its shortlist really is full of smooth Brazilian music — but it had almost no idea which of them would actually sound the yachtiest to a model trained on audio. Curation and judgment turned out to be two different skills.

And a reminder that this measures sound, not quality

Caetano Veloso's "Sampa" — a beloved classic by any measure — scores about 3. That's not a value judgment; it's acoustic MPB, and acoustic MPB simply isn't yacht rock. The model hears a voice and a guitar where the yacht canon has Rhodes, reverb, and a full session band. A low score means "doesn't sound like the target," never "isn't good." If anything, "Sampa" scoring 3 is the model working exactly as intended.

Two models, one playlist: a language model that knew Brazilian music well enough to nominate the right songs, and an audio model that judged how they actually sound. Where they agree, you get "Estrelar." Where they don't, you learn something about both.
